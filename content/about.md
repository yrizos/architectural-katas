Most developers design a system from scratch only a few times in their career. Real projects leave them little room to build that judgment.

At [Epignosis](https://epignosishq.com/), we run architectural katas so everyone on our teams gets that practice far more often. None of it touches production.

The goal is what Gregor Hohpe calls [architecture without architects](https://architectelevator.com/architecture/organizing-architecture/). The teams own their architecture, and when a major decision comes up, anyone on the team can act as the architect for a while.

Hohpe points out that the model works for teams with an architectural mindset and decision discipline, so an organization has to earn it.

The katas are one way we work toward it.

## Why Katas at Tech Events

Inside Epignosis, every kata runs with people who already work together. We wanted to know whether the format holds up somewhere else.

Tech events give us that test, because they change three things at once:

- Most people at a table have only just met, so the team has to build trust while it designs.
- The room mixes far more specialties and experience levels than one company does, so a developer on a first design can share a table with an architect from another industry, and both have to make their case.
- A conference slot cuts the session from two hours to one, so teams have to prioritize faster and leave more questions open.

Ted Neward's [rules](https://www.architecturalkatas.com/rules.html) already ask for the first change. Co-workers may not share a group. Inside a company, people should at least avoid teaming up with those they work with regularly.

At a tech event, that happens without anyone arranging it.
