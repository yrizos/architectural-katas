# How to Participate

## Purpose

This page tells someone taking part in a kata what to do, in what order, what to look for, and what to ask. It works for a team in a room and for one person alone.

The reader leaves able to run a kata without a facilitator. The page proposes phases and gives the questions that move each phase forward.

## Key Information

- What you need. A team of three to five people, paper or a whiteboard, and an hour or two. The same steps work alone, and there is no code to write.
- The brief. The page shows "The Show Must Go On!" in full so the reader has something to work from. What Is a Kata shows it too, and this page uses it as the working problem.
- Three phases.
  - Understand and Prioritize. List the actors and what they do, write down assumptions, pick the top three characteristics, and decide what must ship first and what can wait.
  - Design. Group actions into components, decide what to build and what to buy, choose a style, draw the architecture, and note each key decision on the diagram with the trade-off it accepts.
  - Share. Present your top characteristics, diagram, release plan, and one key trade-off.
- What to look for. Gaps in the brief (no budget, no team size, no existing systems, and "interact with talks" is undefined), assumptions kept apart from facts, and the one open question that could change the design most.
- Questions to ask, grouped under Requirements, Architectural Drivers, Alternatives and Uncertainty.- Vocabulary from the cheat sheet. Ten architectural characteristics (Availability, Scalability, Elasticity, Performance, Security, Maintainability, Testability, Deployability, Interoperability, Cost), six styles (Layered, Modular monolith, Service-based, Microservices, Event-driven, Microkernel), and four product decisions (build or buy, off-the-shelf or open source or custom, managed service or self-hosted, extend an existing system or replace it).
- Phasing. The first release is the smallest scope that delivers value to the main actor. Later releases are ordered by value. The date you must ship by limits what fits in the first release.
- Ground rules. There is no correct answer, only trade-offs. No technology names until the shape is clear. An unstated assumption is a hidden decision.
- What to produce. Decisions with two alternatives and the accepted trade-off, C4 diagrams (context, containers, and components only for the container with the key decision), and a release timeline. The Toolkit page already covers ADRs, timelines and C4, so this page links there and does not repeat it.
- Common mistakes. The page links to Catas to Avoid for the most common kata mistakes.
