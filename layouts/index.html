Practice System Design as a Team

Picture a design project before anything is decided. A few people stand at a whiteboard with a page of requirements. Each would start drawing something different, because each has reasons the others haven't heard.

An architectural kata gives you that situation to practice on. You design a system together, and because nothing ships or breaks, you can try ideas and change your mind freely.

Working with others is the point, because a design nobody questions teaches you little. You start by reading the problem and end by testing your design against a surprise. You finish with a design, the reasons behind it, and a list of what you still don't know. There is no correct answer to find.

Getting Started{{ partial "anchor.html" "getting-started" }}

You'll need a team, which usually means three to five people, plus paper or a whiteboard and an hour or two. The same steps work alone, and there's no code to write. When you're ready, you can work through the same brief in more depth, phase by phase.

  • {{ partial "icons/message.html" }}

    The one rule is that every choice comes with a reason. When someone suggests an approach, the group asks what makes it suitable and what it makes harder.

Keep a page of notes, because each later part builds on it.

Reading the Brief{{ partial "anchor.html" "reading-the-brief" }}

The organizers of a fictional technology conference need a platform to run it each year. Read their brief below once, then again for what it leaves out, because those gaps shape your design as much as the stated needs.

Finding the Gaps{{ partial "anchor.html" "finding-the-gaps" }}

The brief says what the organizers want but not which needs come first. Early-bird ticket sales open soon, yet nothing says whether your platform sells the tickets.

Picture an attendee heading to a room just as that talk moves, then work through these questions to find your priorities:

  • Does the platform sell tickets, or does another system handle that?
  • What must organizers and attendees be able to do by the time ticket sales open?
  • What must keep working when demand peaks or the connection drops?
  • Does interacting with a talk mean questions, polls, feedback, or something else?

Some answers won't be available, such as the budget and the builders. Write down what you're assuming and which choice depends on it, and keep it apart from the brief's facts.

  • {{ partial "icons/flag.html" }}

    Mark the one open question that could change your design most. Return to it when you test your proposal.

Sketching a Design{{ partial "anchor.html" "sketching-a-design" }}

You have priorities, assumptions, and one marked question. The brief raises a choice to compare. Earlier editions could stay in the live platform, or each could be published as a snapshot.

Neither wins by default, because each costs something different:

  • Which of your priorities does each option serve?
  • What would each take to build, run, and change?
  • Which assumption would change your mind if wrong?

Once you lean one way, draw the main parts and label how they connect. Say what each part is responsible for. Then follow a room change and an attendee opening the program through the drawing, and mark what's unresolved.

Write down each decision, the alternatives you considered, and the cost you accept. Those notes turn a drawing into a proposal others can question.

Challenging the Design{{ partial "anchor.html" "challenging-the-design" }}

Check whether your choices hold when the organizers ask for something new.

  • {{ partial "icons/zap.html" }}

    Suppose they add a second venue with its own schedule. That isn't in the brief.

Follow a room change through your drawing again:

  • Which decisions and assumptions does the change affect?
  • Does it make your marked question matter more or less?
  • Do you still accept what your earlier choices cost?

Then ask a teammate or another group what worries them about a choice. Answer by pointing to the need or assumption behind it.

Working alone, revisit an option you rejected. Discussion can show a weakness but can't measure performance, so that claim stays open until someone measures it.

For each choice, decide whether to keep, revise, or investigate it, and write down why. Record what changed in your thinking and what remains open.
