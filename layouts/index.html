Practice System Design as a Team

Real design work usually starts with a request from people who need a system and a date they care about.

An architectural kata is a design exercise that lets you practice that moment with a fictional request, so you can try ideas, have them questioned, and change your mind without any production consequences.

With a small team, you'll read a request from the organizers of a conference, work out what it leaves unsaid, and propose a design. Then you'll test that design against a change nobody planned for. Since there's no correct answer to find, you'll finish with a design, the reasons behind it, and a list of what you're still unsure about.

Getting Started

You'll need three to five people, paper or a whiteboard, and an hour or two, though the same steps work if you're alone. The work is drawing and discussion, so there's no code to write.

The one rule is that every choice comes with a reason. When someone suggests an approach, the group asks what makes it suitable and what becomes harder if you choose it. Those questions are the exercise, so offer your reasons before anyone has to ask for them. Write each one on a page of notes, because every later part of the exercise builds on what you've written down.

Reading the Brief

To have something to design, you need a brief, which is the request written by the people who need the system. This one comes from the organizers of a fictional technology conference, who need a platform to run it each year. Read it once as written. Then read it again looking for what it leaves out, because those gaps will shape your design as much as the stated needs.

The Show Must Go On!

Description

A team of organizers runs a large annual technology conference, bringing together thousands of developers, technology professionals, and industry experts.

Each year features a new lineup of speakers, talks, and activities. The organizers need a platform to manage the conference, keep attendees informed, and support interactions between speakers and participants.

Early-bird ticket sales open on December 1, 2026.

The platform must also maintain an archive of previous editions, allowing visitors to explore past schedules, speakers, and presentations.

Finding the Gaps

Read a second time, the brief says what the organizers want but not which needs come first. Early-bird ticket sales open soon, yet nothing says whether your platform sells the tickets. Questions like that decide what goes into a first release, so work through them and note which needs come first as your priorities.

  • Does the platform sell tickets, or does another system handle that?
  • What must organizers and attendees be able to do by December 1, and what can wait?

Next, test the brief against the day itself, since the show must go on whatever the venue's network does. Picture an attendee heading to a room just as an organizer moves that talk elsewhere, with thousands of phones competing for the same connection.

  • How soon must attendees learn that a talk has moved?
  • What must keep working when demand peaks or the connection drops?
  • Does interacting with a talk mean asking questions, answering polls, leaving feedback, or something else?

Some answers may not be available, such as the budget, the people who will build the platform, and the systems it must work with. For each one, write down what you're assuming and which choice depends on it. Keep these notes apart from what the brief states, so facts and guesses never blur together.

Finally, mark in your notes the one open question that could change your design the most. You'll return to it when you test your proposal.

Sketching a Design

You now have priorities, a set of assumptions, and one marked question, which is enough to start comparing designs. The brief already raises a choice you can use. Earlier editions of the conference could stay in the live platform, or each one could be published as a separate snapshot.

Neither option wins by default, because each costs something different. Your priorities and assumptions decide which cost you'd rather carry.

  • Which of your priorities does each option serve?
  • What would each take to build, run, and change, and who would do the work?
  • Which assumption would change your mind if it turned out to be wrong?

Once you lean one way, sketch the system. Draw its main parts, say what each is responsible for, and label how they connect. Then follow a room change and an attendee opening the program through the drawing to see whether the pieces hold together. Mark anything you haven't worked out so others can see the gaps.

Finally, write down each decision with the alternatives you considered and the cost you accept. Those notes turn a drawing into a proposal, because someone else can now see why it looks the way it does and question it.

Challenging the Design

You've chosen an approach and written down the assumptions behind it. Now check whether those choices still make sense when the organizers ask for something different.

Start with the change, which isn't in the brief, so treat it as a what-if. Suppose the organizers add a second venue in another city, running its own schedule at the same time. Follow a room change through your drawing again and see what the second venue does to it.

  • Which decisions and assumptions does the change affect?
  • Does it make your marked question matter more or less?
  • Do you still accept what your earlier choices cost?

Then ask someone else to question the proposal. Ask a teammate or another group what worries them about a choice, and answer by pointing to the need or assumption behind it. Working alone, take an option you rejected and ask whether it fits better now.

If you disagree about how the system would behave, decide what evidence would settle it. Discussion can show a weakness but it can't give you a measurement, so a claim about performance stays a question until someone measures it.

For each choice you examined, decide whether to keep it, revise it, or investigate it further, and write down why. Keeping a choice is a fine result when it still holds up. Store that reasoning beside the original decision so you can see what changed in your thinking and which questions are still open.
