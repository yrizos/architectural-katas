Practice System Design as a Team

Code gets cheaper every day, and an AI agent can now write a working feature in seconds. Every feature still carries design decisions, and making them on purpose takes practice.

In an architectural kata, your team agrees on what matters, sketches a design, and backs every choice with a reason the others can follow. You leave with the design, the reasons behind it, and a list of what you still don't know.

Why Design Takes Practice{{ partial "anchor.html" "why-design-takes-practice" }}

Most developers design a system from scratch only a few times in their career. Judgment about trade-offs grows with repetition, and a kata gives you far more of it than real work does:

On a Real ProjectIn a Kata
How often you decideA few times in a careerMany times in one sitting
When the consequences show upMonths or years laterWithin minutes, when someone asks why
What a wrong choice breaksA system people rely onNothing, because nothing ships

An architectural kata is a practice exercise created by Ted Neward where your team designs a system that doesn't exist yet from a short brief, with no code to write and no correct answer. You can try an idea, see what it costs, and change your mind as often as the discussion needs.

Why AI Makes Practice Necessary{{ partial "anchor.html" "why-ai-makes-practice-necessary" }}

Writing code used to be slow enough that design decisions came up along the way. Now an AI agent can write a working feature in seconds, and that feature answers questions nobody put to it:

  • {{ partial "icons/archive.html" }}

    Where does the data live?

  • {{ partial "icons/zap.html" }}

    What happens when a call fails?

  • {{ partial "icons/puzzle.html" }}

    What will the next change have to touch?

If nobody answers them on purpose, the code answers them anyway, and nobody notices until the answer costs something. You can no longer afford to forget about architecture, because the code no longer gives you time to notice it's missing.

It works the other way too, when those questions are answered on purpose. At Epignosis, on a codebase that serves more than 20 million users, years of deliberate architecture work left boundaries that AI agents now follow when they add a feature. Without a clear architecture, AI only helps you make mistakes faster.

That changes what you need to get good at. Kent Beck puts it this way in Programming Deflation:

In a world of abundant cheap code, what becomes scarce? Understanding. Judgment. The ability to see how pieces fit together. The wisdom to know what not to build.

That judgment comes from seeing which decisions a change involves and weighing what each option costs. A kata is where you practice it, with a team that asks you to explain every choice.
